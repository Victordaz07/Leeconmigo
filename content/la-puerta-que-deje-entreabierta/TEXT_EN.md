# The Door I Left Ajar

A story for readers ages 10 to 13. Narrator: Isa, age 12.

## Chapter 1 — The Girl Who Made Stars

### Page 1
When I was seven, Mom and I made paper stars at the kitchen table. She folded one point and I folded the other. If a star came out crooked, we said it was dancing. I told her absolutely everything: what I had dreamed, what frightened me, even the boring parts of movies. Bella, my mom, said that someday she would miss all my questions. I didn't believe her. I thought it would always be the two of us and our table covered with stars.

### Page 2
Five years later, the table was still there, but I didn't want to tell her the same things. I had started a photography project with my friend Marisol. My pictures weren't coming out the way I imagined, and I didn't even know if I wanted anyone to see them. When Mom asked, “How was school?” I said, “Fine.” She asked what “fine” meant. I put on my headphones, though I wasn't listening to music. I felt mean as soon as I did it. I also hated feeling as if I owed someone a report on every thought in my head.

### Page 3
Mom tried other ways to get close. She left sliced fruit beside my notebook and offered to look over the pictures. I said I didn't need help. Later she appeared at my door to remind me we were having dinner at Aunt Nora's. “You might want a sweater,” she added. I answered as if she had given me a terrible order. Then I saw the sweater on my bed and was annoyed that she had been right: it was cold outside. Part of me wanted her to take care of me. Another part wanted to decide for myself when I needed help.

### Page 4
At Nora's house, someone found a picture of me with two pigtails and a towel for a cape. Mom told everyone how, at seven, I had announced I would save every sad person with a hug. My cousins laughed kindly. I had done more embarrassing things that very week, but this story made my face burn. It was as if everybody could see a version of me I hadn't chosen to show. “Mom, stop,” I said. She smiled, thinking I was joking, and finished the story. I left my dessert on the table and went into the hall.

## Chapter 2 — What I Didn't Say

### Page 5
On the way home, Mom said nobody had been laughing at me. I said that didn't change anything. “You only know the kid I used to be,” I blurted out. I saw her grip the steering wheel. I didn't want to take the words back, but I hadn't meant to hurt her like that. In my room, I closed the door until only a crack remained. Mom stopped on the other side. “I should have asked before telling that story,” she said. I watched the light beneath the door and didn't answer. Her apology was real. So was my embarrassment. Both could exist at once.

### Page 6
On Monday I arrived late to our project meeting. Marisol had chosen three photographs for the exhibition, and I replaced two without asking her. I was sure mine showed the neighborhood murals better. She looked at the screen and asked if her work counted for anything. “I was only trying to make it better,” I said. I recognized the sentence the moment it left my mouth: it sounded far too much like “I only wanted to help.” Marisol put away her camera. She didn't shout. That made it harder to pretend the problem was hers.

### Page 7
I spent the afternoon writing a perfect message to Marisol. I deleted it eleven times. One version sounded like an excuse; another sounded like a confession before a judge. I wanted to tell Mom, but I pictured a list of advice and a phone call to Marisol's mother. I turned my phone face down. Then I saw one of our old pictures: two shadows held the same camera. You couldn't tell who had decided to press the shutter. Maybe that was exactly the problem. I had treated a shared project as though it belonged only to me.

### Page 8
I found Mom washing two mugs. “I need to tell you something,” I said, “but please listen before trying to solve it for me.” Bella turned off the tap. She sat down and put her phone away. I told her about the pictures and Marisol's face. Mom opened her mouth once and closed it again. When I finished, she asked, “What do you think your friend needs to hear?” It wasn't the answer I had rehearsed arguing against. I said Marisol needed to know her work mattered and that she could choose with me. Mom nodded. “You can tell her that yourself.”

## Chapter 3 — A Different Kind of Door

### Page 9
The next day I asked to talk to Marisol. I didn't begin by explaining why my pictures were good. “I replaced yours without asking. I'm sorry,” I said. She took a while to answer. She had chosen a picture of a damaged wall because the mural wasn't finished: it showed neighbors working together. I had preferred a pretty, completed wall. We looked at both versions. We decided to include both and write together about why we had chosen them. Marisol didn't say everything was forgotten. She passed me the camera so we could check the frame. It was a beginning.

### Page 10
That night I asked Mom why she had told the story about my cape. “Because I love that little girl,” she said. “And sometimes I forget to ask if you want other people to meet her that way.” I explained that I didn't want to hide all our memories; I just wanted a say in which of my stories were shared. She asked me to tell her when I needed space instead of making her guess. We made a small agreement: she would knock before entering and ask before sharing my stories. I would try saying, “Not now, but later,” instead of vanishing behind my headphones.

### Page 11
On Saturday we went out to photograph murals for the project. Mom brought water; I chose the route. In front of a blue wall, she told me that at my age she had also said “fine” when she couldn't explain a difficult day. She wasn't saying it to win an argument. For the first time, it occurred to me that Bella had been a twelve-year-old girl before she was my mom. I handed her the camera, and she took a crooked picture. “It's dancing,” I said. We laughed about the paper star neither of us had mentioned in years.

### Page 12
Back home, I went upstairs to choose the pictures with Marisol. Mom didn't come in when she saw my door ajar: she knocked and waited. “Want to see one?” I asked. I showed her the crooked photo, then another I still didn't like. I didn't tell her every thought I had; some still belonged only to me. She didn't need to know them all to sit beside me. When I was little, I thought growing up meant moving away. Now I was beginning to understand that I could also learn a new way to come close.
