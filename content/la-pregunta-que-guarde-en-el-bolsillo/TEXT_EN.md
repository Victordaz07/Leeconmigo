# The Question I Kept in My Pocket

Narrator: Amara, age 11. For readers ages 10 to 13.

## Chapter 1 — Waiting for a Voice

### Page 1
Grandma Celia says you can talk to God anywhere. I chose my room because nobody interrupts me there, except the fan that sounds like a tired helicopter. I wrote a question in my pocket notebook: “Can you hear me?” I asked it aloud, very softly. I waited. The fan kept spinning. I wasn't expecting the ceiling to open or anything, but I did hope for some way to know my words had arrived. I slipped the notebook into the pocket of my overalls so I could carry the question with me.

### Page 2
At school I carried questions I never said aloud, too. For example, why is it so easy to talk at home and so hard when everybody is watching me? Our teacher had asked us to design, in teams, a map of places at school where a student could feel welcome. Gabriel, who had just arrived, suggested adding the library and a quiet corner of the playground. I thought it was a great idea. When he raised his hand to explain it, I prepared a sentence to support him. The sentence stuck behind my teeth.

### Page 3
Gabriel spread his map across a table. There were colored paths, a bench beneath a tree, and a doorway leading to a place to rest from the noise. Two classmates laughed. One said it looked like a map of a secret base. Gabriel answered that nobody should need a secret base to find a safe place. He looked at me for a moment, perhaps hoping I would say what I'd said while we worked. I looked at my shoes. Nobody had forbidden me to speak. That was the part that hurt most.

### Page 4
On my way home, I squeezed the notebook in my pocket. I wanted to write, “Make Gabriel stop being angry with me,” but that question didn't tell the whole truth. I had been afraid they would laugh at me, too. During snack, Grandma asked how the presentation had gone. I said, “Normal,” in a voice so unlike normal that she raised an eyebrow. She didn't press me. She left a glass of water beside me. Adults sometimes ask too much; that afternoon I was grateful Celia knew how to wait. I still didn't know how to tell the part where I'd stayed silent.

## Chapter 2 — Silence Has Questions, Too

### Page 5
That night I sat on the rug. “God,” I said, “today I saw them laugh at Gabriel, and I was afraid. I didn't laugh, but I didn't stand beside him either. Can you hear me even when I haven't done the right thing?” I waited again. I didn't hear a voice. A car passed outside, and the fan made its helicopter noise once more. I wrote down what I'd said without changing it into a version where I sounded braver. On the next page I wrote, “How do I tell an answer from an idea of my own?” I couldn't answer myself. I left the notebook open.

### Page 6
The next day I told Grandma what had happened, including my question about God. Celia set her cup on the table. “I won't pretend I know exactly what He answered you,” she said. That surprised me. I'd thought grown-ups always had an answer ready. She asked what I knew for certain. I said Gabriel had meant what he said, I'd wanted to support him, and I hadn't done it. “Then you have something honest to tell him,” she replied. She didn't promise Gabriel would forgive me. She offered to practice the conversation with me, if I wanted.

### Page 7
We practiced three times. The first time I sounded as though the other children's laughter were the only problem. The second time I said my silence didn't matter because Gabriel was strong. The third time I managed to say, “I left you alone when I could have spoken.” Hearing it embarrassed me. Grandma didn't erase the sentence. “That part is yours,” she said. I still didn't know whether God had spoken to me. I knew a prayer couldn't take the place of an apology. I put away my notebook and decided to find Gabriel after class, when I could hear his answer without an audience.

### Page 8
I found him closing his folder. Before I could begin, he said, “I don't need you to fight for me.” I stood still. He added, “But you were on our team. You said the playground corner was a good idea, and then you looked at the floor.” He was right. I told him I'd been afraid. I didn't use it as an excuse. “I'm sorry,” I said. Gabriel leaned against the table. “I don't know if I want to present with you all again.” I wanted to say everything would be fine. Instead I asked what he wanted to do with his map.

## Chapter 3 — A New Question

### Page 9
Gabriel wanted to keep the quiet corner. “It's not for hiding anyone,” he explained. “It's so someone can decide whether they need company or a minute alone.” He showed me a path I hadn't noticed: from the entrance to the library without going through the noisiest hall. I asked if I could help check the routes, but made it clear the map was still his. He said he would think about it. That wasn't a yes. It was also better than a promise made just to help me feel less guilty.

### Page 10
The next day Gabriel placed the map between us. He asked me to check whether the playground bench was shady at recess. We went to look and found that the shade moved; we drew another option beneath the big tree. During the new presentation, Gabriel explained the idea and I added what we'd observed. Nobody called it a secret base again. If someone had, I had a sentence ready and was willing to say it. When we finished, Gabriel picked up the map with a small smile. Our friendship was still finding its way.

### Page 11
I showed Celia the notebook but covered one page with my hand. “This part is mine,” I told her. She smiled and didn't try to read it. I told her I'd apologized and Gabriel had decided to work with me again. “Was that God's answer?” I asked. Grandma looked out the window for a moment. “I can't name every part of what you experienced,” she replied. “You can keep talking to Him and pay attention to what you're learning.” I liked that she didn't tie my question shut with a pretty bow. Some questions need more than one day.

### Page 12
That night I sat on the rug again. The fan still sounded like a tired helicopter. I opened my notebook and wrote, “I still don't know how to recognize all Your answers. Today I told the truth, listened to Gabriel, and did something I was afraid to do yesterday.” I didn't know if those words were a conclusion or the beginning of another conversation. Then I said softly, “I'm here again.” I didn't wait for the sky to change color. I left the notebook open, because there was room for more questions.
