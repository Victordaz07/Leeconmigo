# The Map of the Missing Doorway
4459 palabras de narración. Cinco capítulos, veinte páginas.

## 1. A doorway that is no longer there

### Página 01: A map between the pages

Inés was sorting her grandmother's books when a folded sheet slipped onto the table. Her cousin Teo stopped stacking stories and came over.

It was a map, with pencilled streets, a round tea stain, and a red circle beside a tree.

“Treasure,” Teo announced. “It has to be.”

Inés recognised the fountain in the square. Below it was Grandma's house, complete with three windows and its little courtyard.

One detail puzzled her. The drawing showed a doorway where she remembered nothing but a wall.

Teo searched the corners for a date. There wasn't one. Nothing explained the red circle either.

“We could ask Grandma,” he suggested.

Inés glanced towards the courtyard, bright with afternoon sunshine.

“Let's see what else matches first.”

They carefully opened out the map and headed outside. Perhaps the best way to understand what was on the paper was to look beyond it.

### Página 02: The missing doorway

Outside, Teo held the map while Inés checked it against the courtyard. The three windows matched. So did the corner of the roof and the position of the orange tree.

“The door should be here,” he said.

There was only a wall.

Inés moved closer. Beneath the worn plaster, she noticed a curve of bricks. Her eyes followed it down both sides, all the way to the ground.

“Look. Doesn't that seem like an arch?”

Teo glanced up. Once she pointed it out, he could see it too.

“So the map was right!”

“That part fits,” said Inés. “We still don't know what the circle means.”

They listened. Something small dropped on the other side of the wall. An orange, perhaps.

Inés had always thought she knew this courtyard. Now one of its walls seemed to be holding a question.

They folded the map and went to find Grandma.

### Página 03: What a map can keep

Grandma Elena was sitting near the orange tree. When she saw the paper, she traced the round stain with a fingertip and smiled.

“That cup left its mark a very long time ago.”

She went indoors and returned with a box of photographs. In one picture, the doorway stood open, with a garden beyond it.

“Several families looked after it together. I drew this map when I was about your age, Inés.”

Teo pointed at the red circle. “Did you bury something here?”

Grandma shook her head. “That was our meeting spot. Every family brought different seeds.”

Inés studied the photo. The tree looked small. Now its branches reached above the wall.

“Is the garden still there?”

“I don't know. I haven't been inside for years.”

Teo looked at the circle again. He was no longer imagining a pile of coins. “Then we should find out.”

Grandma handed them the photograph. They would have two clues to follow this time.

### Página 04: A wall after the rain

Inés held the photograph up to the bricked-up arch in the courtyard. She wanted to know what had happened, but Grandma Elena took a moment to answer. She touched the creased edge of the map.

“There was a terrible storm. Water rushed through the arch and loosened part of the wall. The grown-ups bricked it up so no one would try to cross while they worked.”

Teo glanced towards the courtyard.

“So nobody closed the garden to hide treasure.”

“Not as far as I know,” Grandma said, smiling. “My friend Ema moved away soon afterwards. I stopped visiting and gradually came to think the garden was gone.”

Inés laid the photograph beside the map. The opening had certainly existed. Some of its curved bricks were still there. Neither clue, however, showed what lay beyond the wall today.

“There might be another way in,” she said. “We should find it, then ask before going through.”

Grandma let them take the photograph. It gave them something more useful than a guess: a way to check their ideas.

## 2. A route we could not see

### Página 05: Two routes

The next morning the cousins set out with their map, the photograph, and a pencil. Teo wanted to race straight to the wall. Inés asked him to begin at the street corner instead.

“If we face the wrong way, every part of the drawing will look wrong.”

They found two things that had stayed put: the sloping edge of a roof and the three windows of Grandma's house. They turned the paper until both landmarks lined up.

Then they noticed a narrow lane drawn behind the garden. Grandma's courtyard gave no view of it; the houses stood in the way.

“So that bricked-up door wasn't the only route,” Teo said.

“It wasn't the only route back then,” Inés replied. “We still have to find out whether this one exists now.”

They reached a fork. Shops and bicycles filled one direction. The other was a slender passage between rows of houses.

Teo pointed to a line continuing beyond a crease in the map. This time they agreed on their next move. Side by side, they followed the passage.

### Página 06: A misleading fold

The lane seemed shorter on paper. Flowerpots crowded its edges. Bicycles leaned against walls, and new doorways opened where the old map showed only yards. The cousins kept to the public path.

Teo counted rooftops in the photograph: one, two, three. He could see four from the far end of the lane. He counted again.

“Did a house appear out of nowhere?”

Inés flattened the map. A deep fold ran across two lines. Between them sat a tiny courtyard that Teo had mistaken for part of the street.

“No missing house,” she said. “We read two lines as one.”

They noted only what they could actually check. A wall had narrow windows like the ones in the photograph. Farther on, orange branches reached above a roof. That did not prove they had found the garden; several homes grew oranges.

Teo suggested measuring the distances in footsteps. It took eighteen steps from the house corner to a spot where they could see the orange branches, and many more to the arch. Pots forced them to turn along the way. Inés wrote the numbers on their separate sheet. A different route would change the count, so it could describe today's walk without silently changing Grandma's old drawing. Teo began to see why she had brought a fresh page: new evidence needed room of its own.

A blue gate appeared at the end of the passage, nearly hidden by leaves. Teo moved towards it, then saw Inés looking at the nearby houses.

“Let's find out whose place it is before we go farther,” she said. “A clue doesn't give us permission to enter.”

A window opened nearby. An older neighbour was watering his plants. Teo lowered the map and said hello.

### Página 07: The gate among the leaves

The neighbour introduced himself as Samuel. He listened carefully, though at first he thought the photograph showed another family's home.

“Look at the roof,” Inés said, pointing to its sloping corner. “Is this the lane?”

Samuel held the picture close to his glasses.

“Yes. They repaired it after the storm years ago. That blue gate leads to a garden. People once entered from Elena's courtyard too.”

Teo raised the map in delight, but Samuel went on.

“I don't know who tends it now. It's been a while since I spoke to the person with the key.”

They thanked him. His answer gave them a route, not an invitation. At the gate, they stopped without pushing it. Fresh paint covered one hinge.

“Someone's been working here,” Teo said.

“Or someone painted a hinge,” Inés answered.

Samuel overheard and laughed. The cousins looked again. A well-used little path led to the other side of the gate. Paint and footprints were different clues, but both raised the same question: who had been coming through?

### Página 08: What we cannot see

Teo knocked once on the gate. They waited. No one answered, though something moved among the leaves. It might have been a person. It might have been branches shifting in the breeze.

“We could peek over,” Teo suggested.

“We could come back when someone's here,” said Inés.

He put his hands in his pockets. He did not want to give up, but even a peek would leave many questions unanswered.

From the lane they could see wet ground and a coiled hose inside the gate. To Teo, that proved the whole old garden was still there.

“It proves somebody watered something,” Inés said. “We can't see how much of the garden in the picture remains.”

The photograph showed an open doorway, a young tree, and a bench near the red circle. None of those were visible from where they stood.

Inés asked Teo for one thing they had observed and one thing they had only imagined. The ground was wet, he said; the old bench still standing there was a guess. Inés admitted that she had taken a moving shadow for a person, though she had not seen anybody clearly. Both were surprised by how readily their minds filled an unseen space. Before they left, they made sure the gate remained shut and slipped the photograph into a folder to protect its edges.

They decided to visit the neighbourhood library. Grandma remembered that families had donated photographs after repairs to the street.

Back at home, Teo drew the blue gate on a clean sheet of paper. He kept the new drawing apart from the old map so yesterday's evidence and today's discoveries would not become mixed up. Inés wrote the date on their sheet.

“Now we have two maps,” she said. “One from then, and one we're making ourselves.”

## 3. Three photographs

### Página 09: Photographs in order

The librarian brought out an album of neighbourhood pictures. In one photograph Grandma's courtyard arch stood open. In another, taken after the storm, bricks already filled the opening.

Teo placed the pictures side by side. Inés found the same roof in both. Plants were still growing beyond the closed doorway in the later image.

“So they didn't abandon the garden when they closed the arch,” she said.

The librarian found a newer photograph, showing the blue gate from the street. Far behind it, the orange tree had grown much taller.

None of the pictures revealed the position of the red circle today. But lined up by date, the photographs showed a change the old map could not explain on its own.

“These aren't three views of one afternoon,” Teo said, revising his first idea.

Inés smiled. Someone had continued looking after the garden. They still needed to meet that person and find out whether the mark on the map meant anything now.

### Página 10: Someone at the gate

The librarian let them copy dates onto their new sheet. They studied the photographs at the table and put the album back where it belonged.

On their way to the lane, Inés reviewed what they knew. The opening had been blocked for safety. The garden had remained green afterwards, at least for a time. A more recent picture showed the blue gate.

“So Grandma was wrong to say it disappeared,” Teo said.

“She said she thought it had,” Inés replied. “She hasn't been inside for years. We thought a red circle might mean buried treasure, remember?”

Teo slowed down. He didn't enjoy the comparison, but he knew she was right. A guess could begin an investigation; it didn't have to end one.

Before knocking, they thought about what to ask. Teo's first idea was, “Where did Ema hide her things?” Inés offered another: “Does someone still look after this garden?” Discovering a route did not entitle them to anybody's private memories. Samuel had shared what he knew; the person behind the gate could choose what to tell them. Teo folded the map so only the lane was visible. If she recognised it, they could show the rest later.

They knocked at the gate. This time they heard footsteps. A woman came through the leaves, soil on her apron and a key in her hand.

“Hello,” said Inés. “We're Elena's grandchildren from the courtyard behind this wall. May we ask you about the garden?”

The woman looked first at Teo's map, then at the old photograph. Her expression changed when she recognised the arch.

“You can come in when I open up,” she said. “I'm Nora. My mother kept a picture like that.”

Teo glanced at Inés. At last they had found someone who might tell them more, though they had no idea what story she would share.

### Página 11: The other entrance

Nora opened the blue gate. Before inviting them through, she asked the children to fasten the latch behind them. They did, leaving the neighbours' bicycles untouched.

Narrow paths crossed the garden. Pots lined the edges, and an orange tree rose above the wall. From this side they could see the old entrance: a curved arch filled with newer bricks.

“That's the same one,” Teo whispered.

Nora held their photograph beside the wall.

“My mother talked about Elena often. They looked after part of this garden together when they were young.”

Inés showed her the red circle. Nora studied the map without answering immediately.

“So much has changed. Show me where the person drawing it would have stood.”

Teo saw the difficulty. From Grandma's courtyard, visitors once walked in facing the orange tree. From the blue gate they arrived in the opposite direction. Turning the paper without checking the arch could put the red circle on the wrong side of the path.

The cousins stood by the wall. They had found the garden, but finding its gate did not mean they had understood the map.

### Página 12: The garden today

The garden did not look exactly like the photograph. Its wooden bench now stood in different shade. Pots occupied the spot where Teo expected a fountain, and a new table by the gate held clean tools.

Nora explained that families had replanted after cold winters. People still came to water. The old entrance had stayed sealed because that stretch of wall needed to remain safe.

Teo searched for a bright red circle. He found a round flowerpot painted red, but Inés noticed its glossy finish. The map was much older.

“Perhaps they moved the pot,” Teo suggested.

“Perhaps. What could we compare that wouldn't move so easily?”

They found the curve of the arch, a wall corner, and the broad base of the tree. Nora let them use the paths and showed them where new shoots were growing.

The tree stood farther from the wall than Inés had guessed from outside. The photograph showed a younger tree but did not prove it was the same one. Nor did it prove that the bench had always stood here.

Inés drew the arch in her notebook, followed by today's tree and the approximate positions of the pots. She left the old bench out. Teo pointed at the empty space; he thought the drawing looked unfinished. It was unfinished, she told him. They did not know where the bench had stood before. Nora liked that answer. An honest blank could be more useful than a neat mark placed in the wrong spot. Teo took the pencil and put a small question on their new sheet beside the gap.

They sat for a while. Their progress felt too slow to Teo.

“We found the garden. How can one painted mark be harder?”

Nora brought them water.

“Places change while people keep living in them,” she said. “Look for something that lasts longer than paint on a pot.”

## 4. The red circle

### Página 13: Ema’s seeds

Nora took a small box from the table and opened its lid. Inside were seeds of many shapes, sorted into little envelopes she had made. Some were as smooth as beads. Others lay flat like coins.

“My mother, Ema, saved some when she moved away. She taught me to grow them, and years later I was able to help look after this place again.”

Teo asked whether the box was the map's treasure. Nora smiled and shook her head.

“It matters to me. But Elena drew the circle long before this box existed.”

Inés set the map beside the photograph. The arch and orange tree could help them find their bearings in both. The box explained who had kept the plants growing. It did not show where the families had met.

Nora gave each child a seed to examine, asking them not to plant it just yet. Teo rolled his between his fingers.

“We thought nothing was left here,” he admitted.

“Other people kept working,” Nora said. “Now we can add our part.”

Inés looked down. Between newer pots, she could make out the edge of a faded red stone.

### Página 14: A circle beneath the leaves

The red stone was nearly hidden under soil. Inés did not pull it out. She asked Nora if they could sweep aside some loose leaves. More stones of the same faded colour appeared beneath them.

They did not form a straight line. Teo stepped back and spotted a curve running towards the orange tree.

“A circle,” he said.

“Part of one,” Inés replied. Pots covered the far side.

Nora moved the lighter pots. The rest of the stones formed a worn ring around a patch of earth. In the old photograph people's shoes hid the ground. On the map, Elena had drawn the mark clearly.

Teo put a finger on the inked circle. Inés aligned the map with the wall's arch first, then the corner of the house. The mark fell near the stones, though not precisely on them. A hand-drawn map was no measuring instrument.

Nora remembered noticing the stones while preparing a flowerbed. She had not put them there. Teo was tempted to call that proof, then realised other stone patterns might exist nearby. They looked around without digging anything up. The arch, the steady wall corner, and the mark's place on the map made their explanation stronger together. What the person drawing the map had meant by the circle still required a conversation with that person.

Inés remembered their first morning. They had faced the drawing without thinking about the direction from which it had been made. Teo had imagined treasure. She had nearly assumed today's tree must be the one in the photograph.

“It's probably the place,” she said. “Let's ask Grandma whether she remembers these stones.”

Teo crouched at the edge without stepping inside. There was no hidden lid or locked compartment. Someone had made a place for people to stand together. For the first time, that seemed better to him than the coins he had pictured.

### Página 15: The mark that remained

They returned to the arch for another look. Nora held the old photograph, Teo the map, while Inés pointed towards the ring of worn stones.

From there they could see what time had changed. A newer bench stood near the table, and the red pot belonged by the gate. Yet the stone circle remained the same distance from the corner of the wall.

“You were right to look for something round,” Inés told Teo. “We just needed to find what was there when the map was drawn.”

He raised the photograph to eye level.

“And you were right about the doorway. That didn't mean you knew everything behind it.”

They laughed together. Nora explained that families sometimes used the ring to exchange cuttings and seeds. It had survived the storm, the repairs, and many smaller changes.

“My mother wanted people to keep gathering here,” she said.

Inés thought of Grandma gazing at her courtyard from the other side of the wall. Just a short walk away, a familiar custom had carried on without her. The children had an answer to bring back. They still wanted to know why no one had told her.

### Página 16: A safe route

The cousins did not try to remove the bricks. Nora explained that the wall had been inspected and that the old opening needed to stay closed. People could enter safely by the blue gate. Finding a place did not mean they had to use exactly the old route.

Teo wrote that on their new sheet. He had drawn two paths. One ended at Grandma's old doorway. The other went around the block. Both reached the garden, but only one worked today.

Before leaving, they asked whether they could tell Grandma what they had found. Nora was delighted. She pointed to the seed box.

“Tell her we still grow some of Ema's plants.”

Inés looked up at the name.

“Grandma thinks Ema left and never heard about the garden again.”

Nora paused. She had been small when her mother moved, and she did not know every reason. She did remember an album at home filled with garden photographs. Ema took it out whenever someone asked about her old neighbourhood.

“She never spoke as though she didn't care,” Nora said. “Perhaps they simply lost a way to reach one another.”

Inés rehearsed what she would tell Grandma. She did not want to say “you were wrong” as though missing a place were an exam answer. She would describe the plants, the seed box, and stones that might mark the old meeting spot. She would also explain what remained unclear: when the bench and pots had moved. Teo added that they had permission to visit by the blue gate and that the old arch should stay closed. Between them, they prepared an invitation instead of a verdict.

Teo carefully fastened the gate behind them. The mystery of the doorway was nearly solved. The question about the two friends needed a different kind of investigation. On their way home, the cousins agreed they could not claim either woman had forgotten the other before hearing more.

## 5. A different way back

### Página 17: Ema’s album

The next day Nora invited them to see the album her mother had left with her. They sat in the garden beneath a cloudy sky. Its photographs showed little harvests, hands carrying pots, and neighbours laughing inside the ring of red stones.

Young Elena appeared in several pictures. Sometimes she held a watering can; sometimes she listened while Ema spoke. Teo wanted to take the whole album to Grandma, but Nora asked them to choose one picture. The other photographs belonged to other families' memories too.

They came across a folded envelope between the pages. Nora left it closed. Her mother had kept it beside a garden photograph, and Nora wanted to speak with her before showing it to anyone.

“What I can tell you,” she said, “is that my mother still has these pictures. She'll be glad to hear Elena remembers her.”

Inés accepted that boundary. Finding an envelope did not tell them what was inside. They could carry the photograph Nora had offered, share the map, and explain what they actually knew.

A drop of rain landed on the table. The cousins slipped the old paper into a folder. The red circle had led them somewhere real. Other people's memories deserved as much care as the old map.

### Página 18: A returned letter

Grandma Elena listened without interrupting. Inés showed her a photograph of the stones. Teo unfolded his new map and traced the route through the lane.

“So the old doorway is still blocked?” Grandma asked.

“It can stay that way,” Teo said. “The blue gate works, and Nora let us in.”

Elena recognised her younger self in the picture. Then she brought out a box from a cupboard. It held a letter she had once sent to Ema. The post office had returned it: Ema no longer lived at that address.

“I thought she wanted a fresh start, and I didn't want to bother her,” Grandma said. “I never asked whether anyone knew a new way to reach her.”

Inés remembered Nora's album. Both women had held on to the same garden. That did not tell the children exactly what either friend had thought for all those years, but it made forgetting seem much less likely.

“Nora could ask her mother whether she'd like to hear from you,” Inés said.

Grandma picked up a clean sheet. It took a while to begin. At last she wrote that she had seen the stone circle again, and that she would love to know how Ema was doing.

Grandma read her new sentence aloud and rewrote it. It sounded too formal. Inés recalled Ema in the photographs: laughing, her hands covered in soil. Elena stopped searching for a perfect opening. She wrote about the first seed they had planted together and a watering can that always leaked. When she finished, she did not ask the children to predict what Ema would feel on reading it. She thanked them for bringing her a route, and a chance to ask for herself.

Teo almost promised a quick reply, then stopped. Letters, like plants, could take time. He could offer something else right now.

“Shall we take you through the blue gate tomorrow?”

Grandma looked from the photo to her grandchildren and nodded.

### Página 19: Coming back

The next morning brought clear skies. Elena followed Inés and Teo along the lane. Once, she stopped to look at the outside of her own courtyard wall. She had never seen it from this direction.

Nora opened the blue gate. Grandma did not search for the old bench. She followed the path until she reached the ring of stones, half shaded by the orange tree.

“It feels smaller,” she said.

“Maybe you got taller,” Teo replied.

They laughed. Elena rested a hand on one worn stone without moving it. Nora showed her the seeds Ema had saved and the album she still cared for.

“Would you ask whether she wants my letter?” Elena said.

“Of course. I'll tell her you came today, too.”

No miraculous answer arrived on the spot. Elena stayed a while, talking to Nora and helping move pots away from the path. Inés realised that returning did not require every detail to have remained unchanged.

When they left, Grandma chose the blue gate. She glanced once towards the sealed arch and smiled. Now she knew how to come back.

### Página 20: Reading beneath the orange tree

With Nora's permission, they brought books to the garden one afternoon. Everyone entered by the blue gate. The old arch stayed safely sealed. Some children settled inside the circle of stones; others read beneath the orange tree.

Teo chose an adventure story. Inés brought the new sheet on which she had drawn the gate and today's route. Grandma kept the original map inside the book where they had found it.

“Do you still think the circle marked buried treasure?” Inés asked.

Teo watched neighbours sharing books and seeds.

“First I imagined coins. Then I thought nothing was there. I got both guesses wrong.”

Nora brought water while Elena read aloud. At the end of a page, the children compared the old drawing with the garden around them. Some things were gone; others had grown.

The letter to Ema was ready. Perhaps an answer would arrive later. For now, Grandma had found a place she could visit again.

Inés closed her notebook without marking the mystery solved forever. Tomorrow there would be new shoots, different stories, and probably more questions. At least they knew a way to ask them together.
