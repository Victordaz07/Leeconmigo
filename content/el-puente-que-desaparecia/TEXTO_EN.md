# The Bridge That Kept Disappearing

Zoe learns to design video games

## 1. A Robot and an Island

Zoe wanted to make video games. For the career fair, she designed one about a little robot carrying seeds to an island.

Her aunt Rosa, a programmer, showed her how to draw the route on paper first. Zoe sketched a shore, two platforms, and a bridge that appeared when a player pressed the correct button.

Leo, her neighbor, wanted to be the first player. Zoe thought he would see the green bridge across the green field right away.

No one else had tested the game yet.

## 2. The First Version

On Zoe’s screen, arrow keys moved the robot. There were two round buttons: a green one opened the bridge, and a red one returned the robot to the start.

Zoe had drawn both without symbols. When she played, she reached the island in seconds and the seeds turned the empty ground into a garden.

“It works,” she announced. Rosa asked whether working in Zoe’s hands meant it was ready for everyone.

Zoe opened the door for Leo.

## 3. The Invisible Bridge

Leo guided the robot to the shore. He looked at the two circles and chose one.

The robot returned to the start. He tried again more slowly, and the same thing happened.

“I cannot tell which button is which, and the bridge gets lost in the grass,” he explained. Zoe almost told him to look harder, but Rosa suggested listening without interrupting.

Leo pointed to the screen: the circles looked similar to him, and the edge of the bridge barely showed.

## 4. Change the Question

Zoe wrote in her notebook, “What did I do wrong?” Rosa gently crossed out the word wrong and suggested a different question: “What does the player need to know what to do?” Zoe realized that color had been her only clue. The game could keep its colors, but it needed more signals.

Leo suggested a bridge icon on the button that opened it and a home icon on the one that restarted the level. Zoe also drew a dark outline around each plank of the bridge.

## 5. A Different Version

The next day, Zoe showed the revised game. Leo recognized the bridge button by its icon without naming a color.

The dark outline made the path visible. He crossed, but the bridge vanished before the robot reached the last plank.

“Now I can see what to do, but it ends too quickly,” he said. Zoe checked her timer.

She had set it for the speed at which she played. Instead of defending her favorite time, she wrote it down as a new problem.

## 6. Take Your Time Mode

Rosa helped Zoe make a mode without a countdown. The main challenge remained for players who wanted it, but Leo could explore without rushing.

Zoe added a visible pause so any player could stop. Leo carried the seeds to the island and played again, this time for fun rather than to prove anything.

Zoe discovered that offering choices did not remove the adventure. It let more people find their own way to enjoy it.

## 7. Another Player, Another Clue

Zoe invited Mina, who often played with the sound very low because she shared a room. When the seeds reached the island, a bell rang, but nothing visible happened right away.

Mina thought the level was still going. Zoe and Rosa watched quietly before explaining.

They added an animation: green shoots rose from the earth and the robot lifted its arms. Now the ending made sense without the bell.

Zoe understood that one test with Leo could not reveal every need.

## 8. The Change List

In her notebook, Zoe organized the comments into a list: recognize buttons, see the bridge, control the time, and understand the ending. Beside each point she wrote what she had changed and who had tested it.

Rosa explained that programming also means revising, listening, and testing again. Zoe could not promise the game would work the same way for everyone, but she could keep asking.

She invited two more classmates and left pencils for them to draw anything confusing.

## 9. The Final Rehearsal

The afternoon before the fair, Leo chose take-your-time mode and Mina turned the sound off. Both saw the icons, found the bridge, and understood when the plants grew.

Zoe tried the timed challenge and had fun. Then a small bug appeared: the pause button did not respond while the robot stood on the last plank.

Rosa and Zoe repeated the steps to find where it happened. They fixed it and tested again.

The game was better because it could still accept corrections.

## 10. The Invitation

Zoe set up her table with a screen, a simple controller, and paper cards showing the icons. She left room for two players and a display that made no grand promises: there was a game to try and comments that could help improve it.

Leo brought a little drawing of an island with different trees. Mina suggested letting the robot choose among several seeds.

Zoe saved both ideas for a future version. She did not have to add them at the last minute to listen sincerely.

## 11. Everyone Crosses

At the school fair, Zoe invited other children to play. Camila chose take-your-time mode.

Samuel noticed that the bridge lights also had clear outlines. Imani found the pause button, and Gael cheered when sprouts filled the island.

Leo explained to a visitor why the icons helped. Zoe thanked each person without claiming her game was perfect.

She had made an adventure, but her favorite part was watching different people find a path through it.

## 12. The Next Level

When the gym was empty, Zoe turned off the screen. She had once thought a programmer was someone who wrote instructions without mistakes.

Now she knew a programmer also made questions for the people who would use what was built. In her notebook she drew another island, but left the bridge blank.

She wanted to hear from players before deciding what it would be. Rosa helped her pack the boxes.

Zoe could already imagine a new level with more than one way across.
