# The Tower That Became a Forest

Gael finds another way to cook

## 1. A three-story dessert

Gael collected recipes the way other children collected stickers. His notebook held invented dishes, family meals, and sketches that resembled edible buildings.

For the school careers fair he wanted to present a dessert that made everyone look up: a three-story tower of soft cake, cream, and fruit. His aunt Amara, a pastry chef, let him work in the community kitchen under her supervision.

His friend Hana would record the tests and help set the table. Gael drew the tower on a sheet so large he had to tape two pages together.

He made the bottom layer the widest and piled gleaming fruit on top. Amara studied the sketch and asked how much weight its top would hold.

Gael said it did not look heavy. Hana wrote the question in the margin.

Nobody had tested the answer yet.

## 2. The testing table

Amara set out the ingredients and handled the oven and any work involving heat. Gael mixed under her eye and compared the sizes of three cake pans.

Hana washed the fruit and dried it carefully while Gael grew impatient to decorate. The first cake came out soft and golden.

It smelled so good that Damián, a boy waiting for his sister at another activity, peeked through the doorway. Amara explained that he could not taste anything before checking with the adult in charge and reviewing the ingredients.

Gael tried to move the large layer while it was still warm. Amara asked him to wait.

“A drawing never melts,” she said, “but cream can change.” Gael watched the clock. If they kept waiting, perhaps the tower would not be ready to photograph before the fair.

He decided the cake was cool enough and reached for the first spoonful of cream.

## 3. A strawberry tremor

The first two layers stood. Gael spread the cream, set the third one above them, and arranged the prettiest fruit on top.

Hana noticed one strawberry slowly traveling down a side. She pointed to another sinking into the cream.

Gael thought he could cover them with more fruit. He added a bunch on the right.

The tower leaned left. Before anyone could touch it, the layers slid across one another and landed in a heap on the clean tray.

Nothing touched the floor, but it no longer resembled a tower. Gael felt his ears grow hot.

Amara set the tray aside to check what could safely be used; Hana rescued the notebook from the spill. In it the perfect sketch remained untouched.

The real dessert had moved in a way his pencil had never predicted.

## 4. The photo he did not want

Gael wanted to close his notebook. The fair would have tables about animals, plants, buildings, and vehicles; his cake pile did not look like a profession.

Hana photographed the tray before he could hide it. “It is a clue,” she said.

Gael almost asked her to delete the picture. Amara offered them two plates: one held cream kept away from the warm cake; the other held a spoonful of the tower’s softened cream.

They could see the difference without tasting it. Hana set three cardboard markers on a sponge to stand for the fruit above.

The sponge gave way. Gael named two possible causes: the cream had softened, and the fruit’s weight was poorly distributed.

Amara did not declare a single answer. She asked what they could change in a small test before using their remaining ingredients.

## 5. Two small towers

Gael and Hana assembled two tiny towers from portions Amara had set aside for testing. Both used the same amount of cake.

In the first they spread cream over a still-warm piece and placed fruit above; in the second they waited for the piece to cool and divided the fruit among lower layers. They left both on the testing table while tidying the kitchen.

The first lost its shape. The second stood, though Gael saw its center move when he touched the tray.

This was not a perfect experiment: they had changed more than one thing between the two. Hana wrote that in the notebook.

“We know this version holds better,” she said, “but not which change helped most.” Gael would have liked to call the mystery solved. Amara smiled when she saw them leave the question open.

She also reminded him that a table crowded with visitors would move more than this quiet one.

## 6. The impossible trip

The fair would not happen in the kitchen. They would have to carry the dessert to the gym, pass through a heavy door, travel up a short ramp, and find a place among other tables.

Gael picked up the test tower and walked slowly around the kitchen. He leaned only a little as he turned a corner; fruit shifted on top.

Hana slipped a tray beneath it before it fell. “We could make the base wider,” Gael suggested.

But time was short, and Amara had reserved enough ingredients for one final batch. They could test another idea, not cook towers until one happened to work.

Gael opened his notebook to the first page and realized he had drawn the dessert standing on a table, never moving. Perhaps the question was no longer how to hold it up.

Perhaps he had to decide what he wanted a visitor to experience while tasting it, even without looking up.

## 7. The clear cup

Hana found clear cups that Amara used for displaying layers. Gael took a portion of cake his aunt had approved for reuse, placed it at the bottom, and added cream and fruit.

It was not a tower: each layer rested against the cup. Hana picked it up, walked around the table, and set it down without anything sliding.

Gael made another cup with less fruit at the top and more color along the sides. The pair resembled tiny gardens seen through windows.

Damián came back and called them “dessert forests.” Gael laughed for the first time since the fall. Amara checked the preparation and storage plan for bringing them to the fair.

Gael could still attempt the great tower, but he no longer wanted to risk his entire table on keeping it perfectly still. He drew six cups in the notebook margin.

## 8. Flavor from the bottom up

Gael thought the ingredients could be stacked in any order. Amara asked him to taste a portion prepared for sampling and describe what came first.

The cream hid the fruit. Gael changed the next cup: cake on the bottom, a little cream, fruit visible against the glass, and another soft layer above.

Hana compared them without looking at his original sketch. The second had different textures in each spoonful.

Gael adjusted the balance so nobody would reach only dry cake at the bottom. His aunt checked the ingredients and kept the dessert properly for serving; Gael did not offer samples on his own.

In the end it was not a smaller tower inside a cup. It was a dessert designed to be eaten from top to bottom, with a small surprise in each spoonful.

## 9. The hallway test

Before calling the work finished, Hana suggested one last test: carrying six sample cups along the route from the kitchen to the gym door. Amara supervised the rehearsal with portions set aside for testing.

Gael held both tray handles; Hana kept the heavy door open. On the short ramp the cups slid together and one tilted.

They stopped before it fell. Gael had solved how to hold each dessert, but not how to stop the cups from bumping one another.

With Amara’s help they added firm dividers to the tray and tried again along a clear path. This time every cup reached its place.

Gael drew the dividers beside his recipe. The detail did not change the flavor, but it changed whether he could share it.

He thanked Hana for imagining the dessert in motion instead of admiring it while it stood still.

## 10. The table shakes

On fair morning a supply cart passed Gael’s table and made it tremble. He watched the cups; their layers stayed in place.

Then other students arrived with models and display boards. Gael left room for Amara to keep the dessert ready for serving and show the ingredient list to the responsible adults.

Hana placed the original tower sketch and the photo of its collapse on one side, the small tests on the other. Gael would rather have hidden the photo, but a child studied the pile of cake and asked how it had become these cups.

Gael described the clues: warmth, weight, and movement. He did not pretend he had planned the forest from the beginning.

The child gently turned one cup and looked through the clear side. “Now I can see it from every direction,” he said.

Gael realized his new dessert told a story too.

## 11. Visitors to the forest

Camila came from her veterinary table with her notebook and asked what had changed after the first test. Imani, showing two ways out of a building, set a cardboard marker at the top of the drawing and asked whether all the cups could travel safely.

Gael showed how they carried them on a steady tray under Amara’s supervision. Abril stopped to study the visible colors and said the cup let her see each layer without taking anything apart.

The fair did not look the same through everyone’s eyes: each child found a different question in the dessert. When it was time for tasting, Amara coordinated portions with the school and families.

Gael listened to responses: one girl wanted more fruit, another preferred less cake. He noted both.

The recipe did not end with the first round of applause; it could keep changing after people tasted it.

## 12. A recipe with two endings

While collecting the empty cups, Gael found the photograph of the fallen tower beneath his notebook. This time he did not want to delete it.

On the next page he drew an arrow toward the first clear cup and another toward a new dessert he might try someday. His aunt asked if he still wanted to be a chef.

“More than before,” Gael said. He had believed cooking meant producing exactly the first drawing.

Now he had watched Amara check what they served, Hana record tests, and visitors discover things he had missed. He loved inventing flavors, but he also wanted to learn to work with real ingredients, real time, and real people.

Damián appeared with the last forest cup, already empty, and asked if they could make a dragon-shaped dessert someday. Gael opened a clean page.

This time he began by drawing how they would carry it to the table.
